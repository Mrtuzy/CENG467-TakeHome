\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We study binary sentiment classification on movie reviews. Given a review $x$, the task is to predict a label $y \in \{0,1\}$ where $0$ denotes negative sentiment and $1$ denotes positive sentiment. The goal is to compare sparse, dense, and contextual representations under a consistent evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Dataset Description}
We use the IMDb dataset \citep{Maas2011imdb} with the official train and test splits, and create a stratified 10\% validation split from the training set (seed 42). The data are balanced across classes.

\subsection{Methodology}
\textbf{Tokenization strategies.} We compare two tokenization strategies and report which model uses each (Table~\ref{tab:q1_tokenizers}). The classical NLTK \texttt{word\_tokenize} is used for TF-IDF and BiLSTM (with the best preprocessing config from Table~\ref{tab:q1_preproc}); the DistilBERT WordPiece tokenizer is used for the contextual model. The two pipelines therefore differ in granularity (whole-word vs.\ subword), in casing handling, and in the way unseen tokens are managed: NLTK reduces them to the unknown token after vocabulary clipping at 30k, while WordPiece falls back to subword units and never emits \texttt{<unk>}.

\begin{table}[H]

\label{tab:q1_tokenizers}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Tokenizer} & \textbf{Granularity} & \textbf{Used by} & \textbf{Test Acc} \\
\midrule
NLTK \texttt{word\_tokenize} & Whole words           & TF-IDF + LR     & 0.8966 \\
NLTK \texttt{word\_tokenize} & Whole words           & BiLSTM + GloVe  & 0.8898 \\

\textbf{Preprocessing ablation (TF-IDF + LR, validation set).} Table~\ref{tab:q1_preproc} reports a 2$\times$2 ablation over lowercasing and stopword removal. Removing stopwords consistently \emph{hurts} validation accuracy, by $0.5$ percentage points when text is lowercased (A2 vs.\ A1) and by $0.4$ points when it is not (A4 vs.\ A3); lowercasing alone has a smaller and slightly negative effect ($-0.08$ pts in A1 vs.\ A3, $-0.08$ pts in A2 vs.\ A4). Configuration A4 (\textbf{no} lowercasing, \textbf{no} stopword removal) is best and is used for the final TF-IDF run. The dominant signal is that bigram features such as ``not good'' or ``never bored'' are valuable for sentiment, so removing common functional words like \emph{not} actively destroys information.

\textbf{Truncation ablation (BiLSTM, validation set).} Table~\ref{tab:q1_trunc} shows that capacity to read the full review matters: extending the sequence cap from 100 to 400 tokens lifts validation accuracy by 5 points. IMDb reviews average 234 whitespace tokens (\S\,Dataset Description), so a cap of 100 truncates more than half of an average review. We therefore use \texttt{max\_seq\_length}\,=\,400 in the final BiLSTM run.

\textbf{Models.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
		\item \textbf{TF-IDF + Logistic Regression} \citep{Pedregosa2011sklearn} (sparse): unigram and bigram TF-IDF features (\texttt{max\_features}\,=\,50k, \texttt{min\_df}\,=\,2, sublinear TF) with linear classifier.
		\item \textbf{BiLSTM + GloVe} \citep{Pennington2014glove} (dense static): word embeddings initialized with GloVe 6B 100d, two-layer BiLSTM encoder (hidden 128), mean pooling, dropout 0.3, linear output.
		\item \textbf{DistilBERT} \citep{Sanh2019distilbert} (contextual): fine-tuned \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} for sequence classification (3 epochs, lr $2\!\times\!10^{-5}$, batch 16).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We use fixed seed 42, a stratified validation split, and evaluate once on the test set. Metrics are Accuracy and Macro-F1. Training time is recorded for each model. Confusion matrices and training curves are saved for analysis.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
We analyze misclassifications with a focus on common error patterns. The counts are: all three models wrong (774), TF-IDF only wrong (863), BiLSTM only wrong (875), DistilBERT only wrong (512). Five representative errors from the DistilBERT test set include:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
		\item \textbf{Negation:} reviews with localized negation often flip sentiment cues (e.g., ``not like this movie").
		\item \textbf{Mixed sentiment:} positive and negative clauses co-occur, making the overall label ambiguous.
		\item \textbf{Domain terms:} film-specific words (e.g., ``plot'', ``acting'') can appear in both positive and negative contexts.
		\item \textbf{Sarcasm:} positive surface words mask negative intent.
		\item \textbf{Subtle tone:} mild criticism or praise without explicit sentiment markers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion}
Sparse TF-IDF features are strong for sentiment cues and offer clear interpretability, but they struggle with long-range dependencies and nuanced phrasing. The BiLSTM benefits from sequence modeling but underperforms TF-IDF in this setup, likely due to limited capacity and the difficulty of long reviews. DistilBERT provides the best performance by leveraging contextual embeddings that capture semantics, negation, and long-distance dependencies more effectively, at the cost of higher training time and lower transparency.
