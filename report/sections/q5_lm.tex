\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We study probabilistic language modeling on WikiText-2. Given a token sequence $w_1, \ldots, w_N$, each model estimates the next-token distribution $P(w_i \mid w_{<i})$ and can generate text by autoregressive sampling. We compare a classical trigram model with interpolated Kneser-Ney smoothing against two neural sequence models: a 2-layer LSTM and a small Transformer encoder LM. Models are evaluated by perplexity on held-out splits and qualitatively through generated continuations of fixed prompts.

\subsection{Dataset Description}
We use the WikiText-2 raw dataset \citep{Merity2017wikitext} (\texttt{wikitext-2-raw-v1}) with the official train/validation/test splits. The corpus is whitespace-tokenized as provided, with no further normalization. Statistics after tokenization are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:q5_data}.

\subsection{Methodology}

\textbf{Trigram with interpolated Kneser-Ney \citep{KneserNey1995,ChenGoodman1999smoothing}.} We train a 3-gram model with interpolated Kneser-Ney smoothing (discount $D = 0.75$). The bigram and unigram backoffs use \emph{continuation} counts $N_{1+}(\cdot\, w)$ rather than raw counts, which reduces the probability of frequent words that nevertheless appear in few distinct contexts. Sentences are obtained by splitting on the period token and bracketed by two \texttt{<s>} symbols and a closing \texttt{</s>}. We implement the count tables and KN scoring directly with Python dictionaries; this avoids the prohibitively slow pure-Python evaluation in NLTK and lets a full validation/test perplexity sweep finish in seconds.

\textbf{LSTM language model \citep{Hochreiter1997lstm}.} A 2-layer LSTM with 256-dim embeddings, 512-dim hidden state, and dropout $0.5$ on both the embedding and the recurrent output. Training uses BPTT with sequence length 35, batch size 64, Adam ($\eta = 10^{-3}$), gradient clipping at 0.25, and a step LR scheduler that halves the learning rate every 2 epochs. We train for 6 epochs with early-stopping on validation loss (best checkpoint reported). The model has 11.37\,M parameters.

\textbf{Transformer LM \citep{Vaswani2017attention}.} A small Transformer encoder LM with a causal attention mask: 256-dim embeddings, 4 attention heads, 2 encoder layers, 512-dim feed-forward, dropout $0.2$, and sinusoidal positional encoding. Training uses BPTT length 35, batch size 64, Adam ($\eta = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}$), gradient clipping at 0.5, the same step LR schedule, and 6 epochs with validation-loss early stopping. The model has 6.18\,M parameters --- roughly half the LSTM's parameter count.

\textbf{Perplexity.} We report token-level perplexity on whole splits:
$$
\mathrm{PPL}(W) = \exp\!\Bigg(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(w_i \mid w_{<i})\Bigg).
$$
Lower is better. For the neural models we use the cross-entropy loss averaged over all non-padding target tokens, which is mathematically equivalent.

\textbf{Generation.} For neural models we use top-$k$ sampling with $k = 20$ and temperature $T = 0.9$. For the trigram model we sample from the top-$k$ continuations of the current bigram context, backing off to a unigram when the trigram context is unseen.

The qualitative pattern is consistent across all four prompts but separates cleanly along two axes that the assignment specifically asks about: \emph{local fluency} (does each adjacent word pair read naturally?) and \emph{global coherence} (does the passage stay on one topic and obey discourse conventions?). The two are not the same and the three models trade them off differently.

\textbf{Fluency.} The trigram produces the most locally fluent surface text. Bigrams such as ``the game 's protagonist as Sim\'on Bol\'ivar'' or ``the equivalent of \pounds 2 @,@ 000 copies'' are word-by-word grammatical and use real proper nouns, because each next-token draw is conditioned on a context that genuinely occurred in training. The LSTM is also locally grammatical (``In the first year of the 19th Kingdom'', ``has been known as \texttt{<unk>}''), but its fluency is degraded by the 10K vocabulary cap: roughly half of its content words are replaced with \texttt{<unk>}, which a human reader registers as broken text even though the underlying structure is sound. The Transformer is the least fluent of the three -- it correctly produces short fragments (``the 19th century BCE'', ``the city 's \texttt{<unk>}'') but interleaves them with raw Wikipedia formatting tokens (``= = = ='') and stretches of consecutive \texttt{<unk>}s.

\textbf{Coherence.} On the global axis the order reverses. The trigram cannot stay on topic for more than its 2-token context window, so a sentence that starts on the meaning of life ends with ``Tech made good the penalty , giving up on the same time'' -- locally fluent, globally incoherent. The LSTM's recurrent state lets it keep a single referent alive across a clause: ``in the early years of the \texttt{<unk>} of \texttt{<unk>} 's reign. In the first year of the 19th Kingdom, the \texttt{<unk>} was a \texttt{<unk>}'' is on a single topic (a king's reign) for two consecutive sentences, even if the named entities are masked. The Transformer also stays on a single topic but is more brittle, often collapsing into the section-header attractor (``= = = =''), which is itself a topic-coherent failure mode -- it recognises that section markers cluster together in WikiText-2 and reproduces that pattern.

\textbf{Why the two axes diverge.} Perplexity rewards calibrated probability mass on the next single token, which mostly measures local fluency on common tokens -- exactly where the LSTM is best ($97.0$ test PPL). It does not directly measure whether topic is preserved across a paragraph, which is why the trigram can read more smoothly word-by-word while still being $3.9\times$ worse on PPL. Conversely, the LSTM's apparent disfluency in the qualitative table is largely a vocabulary artifact: replace each \texttt{<unk>} with the token it stands in for and the LSTM becomes both the most fluent and the most coherent of the three.

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{Why the LSTM wins.} The LSTM's recurrent memory and its larger parameter count let it model the long, comma-separated clauses that dominate WikiText-2, which the trigram --- limited to two preceding tokens --- structurally cannot. The Transformer should in principle do at least as well, but at this scale (2 layers, 6.18\,M parameters, 6 epochs, no learned positional embeddings, no warmup) it is undertrained. Transformer LMs are known to need more data and a more careful optimizer schedule than RNNs to reach their full potential; the gap we observe here ($\sim$20 PPL on test) is consistent with that.

\textbf{Why the trigram is so far behind.} On a $\sim$2\,M-token corpus with a 10K vocabulary, most trigrams in the validation/test sets are unseen in training, so the model spends almost all of its probability mass via Kneser-Ney back-off. Continuation-count back-off is well-known to be a strong baseline, but it cannot capture syntactic dependencies more than two tokens away, and WikiText-2 sentences are long ($\approx$110 tokens on average). The neural models, even at modest capacity, can.

\textbf{Limitations and what would help.} (i) The 10K vocabulary cap creates a heavy \texttt{<unk>} tax on the neural generations; subword tokenization (e.g.\ BPE) would let the same models produce far more readable outputs without changing the perplexity story. (ii) Six epochs is short; longer training with cosine-annealed learning rates would close more of the LSTM--Transformer gap. (iii) Perplexity does not measure factuality or topical coherence, both of which are visibly weak across all three models. (iv) WikiText-2 itself is small enough that count-based methods underperform; on a substantially larger corpus the absolute gap between the trigram and the neural models would grow further.

Overall, on WikiText-2 with the full 6-epoch budget, the parameter-rich LSTM is the strongest model, the small Transformer is a reasonable second, and the Kneser-Ney trigram --- though a useful sanity-check baseline --- is decisively beaten by both neural models.
